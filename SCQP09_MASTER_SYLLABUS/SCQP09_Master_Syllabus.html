Five conclusions that drive the whole plan

  1. Mathematics is the largest single block. 70 of 225 questions. Even in the leaner 2025/2026 papers it is 20 questions — more than any other subject.
  2. TDM is the best return on time. 50 questions, no prerequisites, and 2026 raised it to 20. It is the block where speed can be trained fastest.
  3. The CS core is remarkably stable. DSA + Digital + OS = 104 questions (46.2%), and each paper kept the three between 29 and 40 questions.
  4. 2026 changed the format, not the syllabus. Match-the-list, arrange-the-order and assertion–reason questions increased sharply. Content remained the same five blocks.
  5. The clock is now the main enemy. 90 minutes for 75 questions = 72 seconds per question. The paper rewards recognition and elimination, not long derivations.

Math 20 · TDM 15 · OS 14 · DSA 13 · Digital 13

Section 2

The learning order

The syllabus is not a list to be studied alphabetically. Some blocks unlock others: algebra unlocks calculus; logarithms unlock complexity; digital memory unlocks OS memory; trees and queues unlock OS scheduling and file systems. The order below follows those dependencies and front-loads the highest-weight, most reusable material.

1
Mathematics toolkit
Sets · counting · probability · statistics · algebra · matrices
2
Mathematics core
Coordinate geometry · calculus (limits, continuity, differentiation, integration)
3
Digital Fundamentals
Number systems · Boolean algebra · combinational · sequential · memory
4
Data Structures & Algorithms
Complexity · linear structures · trees · graphs · sorting · hashing
5
Operating Systems
Processes · scheduling · synchronization · deadlock · memory · virtual memory · I/O
6
Integrated revision
Full papers · error log · formula sheet · speed drills
Parallel track: Thinking & Decision Making does not depend on any other block. Start it on day one and do 30–40 minutes daily alongside the core sequence. Do not wait until the end — it is 22% of the paper and the easiest place to gain speed.

Why this order and not the paper order

StageWeeksWhat you studyWhy it comes here

2. Math core4–7Coordinate geometry, calculusCalculus is the single largest math topic (21 questions). Coordinate geometry is second (15).
3. Digital8–9Number systems, Boolean, K-map, circuits, memoryShort, self-contained, high-scoring. Memory basics are needed for OS.
4. DSA10–12Complexity, lists, stacks, trees, graphs, sorting, hashingLogarithms and summation from math make complexity easy; queues/trees are reused in OS.

The six rules behind the sequence

1. Prerequisite first

Do not start calculus before you can solve quadratics, inequalities and functions. Do not start complexity before logarithms and series.

2. Weight before novelty

Math and TDM are 53% of the paper. They get the first eight weeks and daily time, even if OS feels more “interesting”.

3. Short blocks early

Digital is compact and self-contained. Finishing it in two weeks gives an early confidence boost and unlocks OS memory.

4. Numerical before theory

In OS and DSA, learn the formula/algorithm first, then the theory. The paper asks “compute”, “arrange” and “match” more than “define”.

5. TDM daily, not weekly

Reasoning is a skill, not a chapter. Twenty minutes daily beats a four-hour Sunday block.

6. Revise by retrieval

Every module ends with “how it is asked”. Cover the answer, attempt the archetype, then check. Reading notes is not revision.

Stage gates: do not advance until you can…

StageGate test (closed book, timed)
1. Math toolkitSolve a quadratic, a log equation, an AP/GP question and a three-set Venn problem without notes.
2. Math coreDifferentiate and integrate standard forms, find a tangent, and identify a conic from its equation.
3. DigitalConvert bases, fill a K-map, identify a flip-flop, and compute memory capacity from address lines.
4. DSAState the standard complexities, traverse a tree, and explain BFS vs DFS with their data structures.
5. OSCompute WT/TAT, check a safe sequence, translate a paging address, and compare page-replacement algorithms.
6. RevisionAttempt 65+ questions at 80%+ accuracy in a strict 90-minute mock, with a written error log.
If a gate fails, do not restart the stage. Repair the specific module that failed, retest it in 48 hours, and keep the rest of the sequence moving. A stage gate is a diagnosis, not a punishment.

The master syllabus

Each card is one study module. The order inside a subject is the order you should learn it. The three small pills show how many questions came from 2024, 2025 and 2026. The priority badge tells you how much depth to invest.

How to read a card

What to learn is the minimum concept set for that module. If you can explain every bullet and solve one example of each, you are ready for the exam level.

How it is asked is the actual question behaviour observed in the three papers — not a guess.

PYQ signal gives a real question or a close paraphrase so you know the flavour.

Four rules for using the cards

1. Follow the order. Subject order, then card order, is the learning sequence. Jumping to a later card usually means missing a prerequisite.

2. Read the pills first. 24 / 25 / 26 counts tell you whether a topic is a recurring earner or a one-off. A gray pill is not failure — it is information.

3. Test with “How it is asked”. Close the card and answer each bullet from memory. If you can do that, the module is exam-ready; rereading “What to learn” is not revision.

4. Stop at the depth line. P0 gets every bullet, P1 gets the standard pattern, P2 gets one example, P3 gets one page. Spending P0 time on P3 is how syllabi become unfinished.

OS theory traps

PairDistinction the examiner tests
Deadlock vs starvationDeadlock: a set of processes can never proceed. Starvation: a process can proceed in principle but is indefinitely overlooked.
Paging vs segmentationPaging is fixed-size, invisible to the programmer, internal fragmentation. Segmentation is variable-size, logical, external fragmentation.
Prevention vs avoidancePrevention breaks one Coffman condition. Avoidance uses future information (Banker's) to stay in a safe state.
Detection vs recoveryDetection finds the cycle (wait-for graph). Recovery breaks it: terminate a process or preempt a resource.
Internal vs external fragmentationInternal: allocated block is larger than needed. External: free memory is split into non-contiguous holes.
Page fault vs thrashingA page fault is one miss. Thrashing is a sustained high fault rate that leaves no CPU time for useful work.

A 90-minute, 75-question paper with +4/−1 is a triage exercise. You cannot solve everything; you must choose what to solve, in what order, and when to leave. The plan below is built for the actual subject mix.

  2. Digital (10–13 Qs, 11 min).
  3. DSA (10–13 Qs, 13 min).
  4. OS (9–14 Qs, 13 min).
  5. Math (20–30 Qs, 28 min) — attempt the easy 15 first.

The guessing rule (do the maths once)

With four options and +4/−1, a blind guess has expected value 0. If you eliminate one option, EV = +0.33; eliminate two, EV = +1.0; eliminate three, mark it. Therefore:

  • No elimination → leave it. A zero-EV guess adds variance, not marks.
  • One option eliminated → guess only if you are short on attempts.
  • Two options eliminated → always guess.
  • Three options eliminated → never leave it.

Ten instructions for the hall

  1. Read the last line first. It tells you whether the question is “which is true”, “which is NOT”, “minimum”, or “arrange”.
  2. Write the subject and formula before solving. Ten seconds of labelling prevents most silly errors.
  3. Use units as a check. ns vs ms, bits vs bytes, degrees vs radians, sq. units.
  4. Do not re-derive standard results. Recall the limit, the derivative, the complexity, the flip-flop table.
  5. For match questions, fix the sure pairs first. The one-to-one constraint usually finishes the rest.
  6. For arrange questions, find first and last. The middle often follows.
  7. For statement questions, mark T/F in the margin. Do not hold four evaluations in your head.
  8. For numericals, estimate before computing. If the options are far apart, approximation is enough.
  9. Never spend more than 2 minutes on one question in Pass 1. The paper is designed to make you do that.
  10. Keep the last 5 minutes for review. Check NOT questions, marking, and that no easy question is blank.

What a top score looks like

75 questions, 300 marks. A realistic AIR-1 range is 240–270: about 62–68 correct, 3–5 wrong and 4–8 blank. That is not perfection; it is triage plus accuracy. The plan above is designed to get you to 65 attempts with 90% accuracy, not to make you solve every question.

How the 225 questions were classified

  • OCR is reliable for question stems but can garble mathematical notation and Hindi transliteration. Classification was done by reading the question, not by keyword matching alone.
